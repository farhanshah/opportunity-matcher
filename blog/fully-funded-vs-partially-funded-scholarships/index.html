Fully Funded vs Partially Funded Scholarships: Compare the Real Cost

Learn to compare tuition waivers, stipends and uncovered expenses without relying on marketing labels.

OppMatcher editorial · Research and application guidePublished 23 October 2026Updated 23 November 2026 with official sources

Learn to compare tuition waivers, stipends and uncovered expenses without relying on marketing labels. The goal is to check the exact rules of the programme you intend to use, not to treat any general guide as a confirmed open application.

Make a cost-of-study worksheet

List tuition, housing, food, health cover, books, course fees, travel and residence-document costs. Estimate expenses using the intended university and city rather than a generic national average. Then record what the scholarship covers under each category, using the provider's exact wording.

Separate a waiver from cash support

A fee waiver can be valuable but may provide nothing for rent or arrival costs. A monthly maintenance award may support living costs without paying tuition. Check whether a scholarship pays the institution directly, reimburses expenses, or transfers money to the student after enrolment.

Read conditions that affect the total

Some awards have renewal requirements, performance conditions, part-time restrictions or limits on the number of funded terms. A master's award for one academic year may not cover a longer placement or resit. Read the funding conditions rather than estimating from the largest advertised annual amount.

Compare net cost, not the badge

Calculate the difference between expected eligible expenses and confirmed support. Include the first month's costs and any required deposit before payments begin. A smaller award in an affordable city may be more useful than a prestigious tuition discount combined with expensive housing.

Worked example

Example: Scholarship A covers 100% of tuition but no living costs. Scholarship B covers 60% of tuition and offers monthly housing support. A fair comparison requires the local tuition bill, course length, housing cost and payment schedule; neither label alone identifies the better option.

Action checklist

  1. Write the net cost in the host currency
  2. Mark each benefit as confirmed or unknown
  3. Check renewal and payment conditions

Frequently asked question

Does 'fully funded' always cover flights?

No. The specific award terms control travel, insurance and other costs.

"Fully funded" is a description, not a standard

No regulator defines "fully funded". Two scholarships with that label can cover very different things. The only reliable test is the provider's own list of benefits. Here is what three well-known schemes actually list on their official pages:

All three are commonly called fully funded, yet they differ on family support, insurance and visa costs. Each could still leave you with costs to pay yourself, and those are the costs to plan for.

Caps and exceptions hide inside "full" awards

A full award can still have a ceiling. Cranfield University's Chevening page, for example, notes that Chevening applies a £22,000 tuition cap to MBA programmes, so applicants pay anything above it. In Germany, the University of Stuttgart warns that DAAD scholarships do not cover Baden-Württemberg's €1,500-per-semester fee for non-EU students. Neither scheme is misleading: the limits are in the terms. But you only find them by reading the terms, and the course or region you choose can be the deciding factor.

How to compare a partial award with a full one

A partial award, such as a tuition discount, a fixed sum or a fee waiver, can be worth more to you than a prestigious full award you are unlikely to win, or one that ties you to an expensive city. The fair comparison is net cost: your expected total costs minus the confirmed support.

Worked example: a one-year UK Master's

Assume tuition of £24,000 and living costs of £1,300 a month for 12 months (£15,600). Total: £39,600.

  • Offer A, a tuition-only award: pays £24,000. You still need £15,600 for living costs, plus flights and the visa.
  • Offer B, a £10,000 partial scholarship: leaves £29,600 to find.
  • Offer C, a full award with a stipend of £1,712 a month (the Commonwealth rate outside London): covers tuition and pays £20,544 over 12 months, more than the assumed £15,600 living costs. Your remaining risk is costs the scheme does not list.

These figures are illustrations. Replace them with your course's published fee, a realistic rent for the city and the provider's current rates.

Questions to answer before accepting

  1. Does the award pay tuition directly to the university, or reimburse you later?
  2. When does the first living-allowance payment arrive, and what do you need for the first month before it does?
  3. Are there caps on tuition, or on particular course types?
  4. Does it cover your visa, health surcharge or insurance?
  5. Is anything paid for dependants?
  6. Can it be combined with another award, or does accepting one cancel the other?
  7. What happens if you need to extend, resit or switch course?

If the official terms don't answer a question, email the provider and keep the reply. Don't rely on a forum post or an agent's summary. Our eligibility checklist helps you screen schemes before comparing their value.
