International Internship Visa and Work Authorisation: What to Ask

Understand the difference between permission to travel and permission to complete a placement.

OppMatcher editorial · Application guidancePublished 23 October 2026Updated 23 November 2026 with official sources

Understand the difference between permission to travel and permission to complete a placement. Use this guide to evaluate a specific posting against its official evidence; it is not a guarantee that any particular internship or selection process is currently open.

Identify the legal form of the placement

An internship may be treated as employment, training, volunteering or part of an accredited study course depending on local rules. The host organisation should describe the contract and duties accurately. A tourist visa is not automatically permission to perform work.

Ask the receiving institution specific questions

Request the expected start and end dates, paid or unpaid status, work location and any sponsorship documents it can provide. Check the host country's current official immigration guidance for your nationality and circumstances before booking flights.

Include remote cross-border work

Working from a different country can raise employer authorisation, insurance and tax issues even if the organisation calls the internship home-based. Confirm the permitted country of work in writing. Do not infer freedom to work from a virtual interview invitation.

Avoid relying on old social posts

Immigration conditions can change faster than evergreen internship guides. Use government sources for the actual application path and seek qualified immigration advice for complex cases. Keep visa and job requirements separate so you do not mistake an offer for immigration approval.

Worked example

Example: an applicant offered an unpaid placement in the UK should confirm whether the specific activity is allowed under their current immigration permission. The fact that the placement pays no salary is not sufficient evidence of permission.

Before applying

  1. Confirm placement category and location
  2. Use the host government's current rules
  3. Ask for employment and sponsorship documents

Frequently asked question

Does an overseas internship automatically provide visa sponsorship?

No. An employer may accept international applications without arranging work permission. Check the duty station, nationality and residence rules, who handles the visa, and whether a university placement uses a different process.

The pattern almost everywhere: no sponsor, no internship visa

Countries rarely have a visa simply called "intern". Instead, an internship normally fits into a temporary work or exchange category, and that category almost always needs a sponsor: an organisation approved by the government to vouch for the placement. An offer letter from the host company is not enough on its own. Before you accept an international internship, the first question is: who sponsors the visa, and under which category?

United Kingdom: three situations, three answers

Your situationUsual routeKey rules
Coming to the UK only for an internshipTemporary Work – Government Authorised Exchange (GAE) visaYou need a certificate of sponsorship reference number from a licensed sponsor. Depending on the scheme, the visa lasts up to 12 or 24 months. You can apply up to 3 months before the start date.
Studying in the UK on a Student visa, with a placement built into your courseYour Student visaA placement that is an integral and assessed part of the course can be full-time during term. University guidance, such as UCL's, notes that the placement must not take up more than half of the programme.
Studying in the UK, with an internship that is not part of your courseYour Student visa, within its work limitFor degree-level study the limit is usually 20 hours a week in term time, full-time only in official vacations. Universities warn that a remote "virtual internship" for an overseas employer still counts towards the 20 hours while you are physically in the UK.

GAE sponsors include organisations that run approved exchange schemes, universities (for researchers) and government departments. The certificate of sponsorship states your job, start date, hours and how long you can stay. Check that those details match your offer exactly. You can enter up to 14 days before the job starts and stay up to 14 days after it ends, within the dates on your visa.

Postgraduate students should note one trap. For most taught Master's students, the summer dissertation period counts as term time, so the 20-hour limit still applies to summer internships.

United States: J-1 intern and trainee categories

Internships in the US for non-US students usually run through the State Department's Exchange Visitor Program in the J-1 intern or J-1 trainee category. These are run by designated sponsor organisations, not by employers directly. As sponsors such as the American Immigration Council describe the rules:

  • Interns must be enrolled in a degree or certificate programme at a post-secondary institution outside the US, or have graduated from one no more than 12 months before the programme start date. The maximum duration is 12 months.
  • Trainees are for people with professional experience. The maximum is 18 months in most fields, but hospitality training is capped at 12 months.
  • The placement must be directly related to your field of study or occupation. Return intent is a core requirement of the programme.

The sponsor issues the DS-2019 form, and you then apply for the visa at a US embassy or consulate. Sponsors charge programme fees, so ask who pays them before you sign.

International organisations: you usually handle the visa

For UN Secretariat internships, the UN's own page says interns are responsible for obtaining and paying for any visas, and for arranging their own travel. UNICEF provides supporting documents for visa applications when needed. A placement at an international organisation does not usually come with a visa sponsored in the way an employer would sponsor one. Confirm what letter the organisation will issue, then check the host country's visa route yourself.

Worked example: a Master's student weighing two offers

A non-UK Master's student at a UK university receives two summer offers in June: a full-time 10-week internship in London, and a remote role for a company in their home country.

  • The London internship: their Master's runs until September, so the summer counts as term time. Unless the internship is an assessed part of the course, they can work no more than 20 hours a week. A full-time role would breach their visa conditions.
  • The remote role: working from the UK still counts towards the 20 hours. Both roles together must stay within the limit.

Their options are to negotiate part-time hours, defer the London role until after the course officially ends, or ask the university whether an assessed placement module is available. Their university's immigration advisers, not the employer, should confirm the answer.

Questions to ask before you accept

  1. Which visa category does this internship use, and who is the sponsor?
  2. Who pays the sponsor's fees, the visa fee and any health surcharge?
  3. Do the dates, hours and pay on the sponsorship document match the offer?
  4. If I am already on a Student visa, is this an assessed part of my course or does the hour limit apply?
  5. Is any part of the work remote, and from which country?
  6. What happens if the visa is refused or delayed? Can the start date move?

Rules change. Check the official government page for each country on the day you apply, and treat a university or sponsor summary as a guide, not a ruling. See also our guide to UK internships for international graduates.
