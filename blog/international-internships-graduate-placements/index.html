International internships and graduate placements

A good overseas internship is more than a job title in another country. Check candidate status, pay, work rights, dates, supervision and the actual application route before you commit.

OppMatcherUpdated 15 October 2026Official guidance reviewed 15 October 20268 minute read
Quick answerStart by confirming who can apply and whether you can legally take the role. Then compare compensation, location, duration, work format, responsibilities, supervision and application requirements. Use the employer or programme page as the final source.

Internship, placement or graduate programme?

The labels overlap, but the structure can be different. An internship is usually a fixed period of work experience. A placement can be part of a degree or a longer work based period. A graduate programme is normally an entry route for people who have completed a degree and may include rotations or formal training.

Do not rely on the title alone. Read the candidate status and timing rules.

1. Student or graduate status

Some internships require you to be currently enrolled. Others accept recent graduates for a limited period after graduation. Graduate placements may require the degree to be completed before the start date.

If a programme mentions a graduation window, compare it with your actual award date, not the date you attended your final class.

2. Work rights, visa and location

International opportunities can be open globally while still requiring the candidate to secure permission to work in the host country. Other programmes are hosted by international organisations with their own arrangements.

Check whether the employer sponsors, supports or simply expects you to already hold the required work permission. If the listing is unclear, treat this as a question to resolve before spending time on a long application.

3. Pay, stipend and real cost

Paid, funded and unpaid are different. A stipend may help with living costs without matching a normal salary. A funded internship may cover travel or accommodation separately. Always check what the payment is intended to cover and whether relocation costs remain with you.

4. Role fit and learning value

Look beyond the organisation name. Read the tasks, team, tools, outputs and learning goals. A famous employer is not automatically the best match if the work has little connection to the skills you want to build.

For research internships, check the host unit or project area. For policy and development roles, check the actual function rather than assuming every role at the organisation is similar.

5. Dates, duration and availability

Confirm the application deadline, start date, end date and whether dates are fixed. A programme can be open for applications while offering several intake windows.

If you are studying, compare the internship period with exams, dissertation deadlines and university rules. Some universities need to approve placements before they begin.

6. Application documents

International internship applications commonly ask for a CV, cover letter, transcript, proof of enrolment or graduation, and sometimes a writing sample or portfolio. Research programmes may ask for academic interests or a preferred host group.

Tailor the evidence to the role. A general cover letter that could be sent to any employer wastes the strongest part of the application.

For internships at UN agencies, use the UN internship application checklist to confirm the exact requisition, academic status and applicant restrictions before preparing your documents.

7. Check the quality of the opportunity

A useful listing should make the employer or host organisation clear, explain what the intern will do, state the location or work format, and provide an application route you can verify.

Be careful with third party pages that hide the employer, charge for access to a public vacancy, or promise guaranteed placement. If a placement service charges a fee, understand exactly what service you are buying and whether the employer itself is involved.

A practical search routine

  1. Choose the type of experience you want: research, corporate, policy, development, technical or another route.
  2. Filter by student or graduate status.
  3. Check country, work rights and location requirements.
  4. Compare pay and relocation costs.
  5. Review tasks and field fit.
  6. Confirm dates and availability.
  7. Prepare the required documents.
  8. Apply through the official employer or programme route.
