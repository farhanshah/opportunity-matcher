PhD Fellowship vs Research Grant: Funding Differences Explained

Understand whether an award funds your own doctoral study, a supervisor's project or a research institution.

OppMatcher editorial · Research and application guidePublished 23 October 2026Updated 23 November 2026 with official sources

Understand whether an award funds your own doctoral study, a supervisor's project or a research institution. The goal is to check the exact rules of the programme you intend to use, not to treat any general guide as a confirmed open application.

Find the applicant named in the scheme

A doctoral fellowship may assess you as an individual student, while a research grant can be submitted by a principal investigator or university. Read the eligibility section and identify who signs the contract. A scheme for postdoctoral researchers does not automatically fund someone who has not yet earned a doctorate.

Understand what the money actually pays

Look at tuition, salary or stipend, employer costs, equipment, research travel and overheads as separate categories. A laboratory grant worth a large amount is not necessarily personal funding. Confirm whether a stipend is tax-free or a salary subject to local rules, based on the actual agreement.

Check the host and supervision requirements

Funding may depend on an approved supervisor, named project, host institution or particular type of research environment. Contact the potential supervisor with a concise research question, preparation and funding route. Do not promise an institutional commitment the department has not offered.

Work backwards from research milestones

Research proposals, ethics approvals, institutional sign-off and recommendation letters can all delay applications. A doctoral applicant should also know whether admission must come first. Prepare an evidence trail for each requirement and allow a realistic period for the university to approve the application.

Worked example

Example: a candidate interested in climate adaptation may find a PhD studentship advertised by a department and an open postdoctoral grant from the same funding body. Only the first is likely suitable before finishing doctoral training.

Action checklist

  1. Confirm the degree stage required
  2. Record who receives and spends the funding
  3. Check required host and supervisor endorsement

Frequently asked question

Can I use a postdoctoral fellowship to start a PhD?

Normally these are different career stages. Check the specific programme.

Three different things that all get called "funding"

Research funding comes in forms that differ in who applies, who holds the money and what you are paid as:

  • A studentship funds a doctoral student. In the UK it usually covers fees and a tax-free stipend, and you apply to the university or doctoral programme, not the funder.
  • A fellowship funds a named individual to do their own research. For postdocs this is often an employment contract at a host institution, as with Marie Skłodowska-Curie postdoctoral fellowships.
  • A research grant funds a project. It is held by an institution, usually led by an established researcher, and may pay for posts, including PhD or postdoctoral positions, on that project.

MSCA postdoctoral fellowships: two rules that rule people out

The European Commission's MSCA Postdoctoral Fellowships are open to researchers of any nationality, but two conditions are strict:

  • The mobility rule: you must not have resided or carried out your main activity (work, studies and so on) in the host country for more than 12 months in the 36 months immediately before the call deadline. For Global Fellowships, this applies to the country of the outgoing phase. Remote work counts as activity in the employer's country, according to university research offices.
  • Experience limit: you must hold a doctorate by the deadline, and have no more than eight years of full-time-equivalent research experience since your PhD. Some leave, such as parental leave, extends this.

European fellowships typically run 12 to 24 months, and Global fellowships 24 to 36 months. The host must recruit you under an employment contract, or an equivalent with similar social security cover, for the whole period.

The international student fee gap in UK studentships

UKRI pays fees only up to the home rate, so an international student on a UKRI studentship can face a shortfall between that and the international fee. Many universities waive the difference for studentship holders, but not all. Before accepting, ask in writing: will the international fee difference be waived, and for how many years?

Worked example: three offers for the same researcher

A researcher finishing a PhD in Brazil, who has lived there all their life, compares three routes to Europe:

  1. An MSCA European fellowship hosted in the Netherlands: eligible on mobility, since they have spent no time there in the last 36 months. They are employed, with social security, for up to 24 months.
  2. A postdoc post on a UK research council grant: no mobility rule, but the post is defined by the grant holder's project, and they apply as for any job.
  3. A second, UK-funded studentship to switch fields: possible, but it pays a student stipend rather than a salary, and the fee shortfall question applies.

If they had spent 14 of the last 36 months in the Netherlands on a visiting position, option 1 would be closed for a Dutch host, though not for a host in another country.

Research funding checklist

  1. Identify whether the scheme funds a student, a person or a project.
  2. Check who applies: you, your supervisor or the institution.
  3. Check the mobility and experience rules against your own dates.
  4. For UK studentships, confirm the fee position for international students.
  5. Confirm whether you would be a student, an employee or neither, as this affects tax, social security and visas.
