How to Write a Research Proposal for a Scholarship

Turn a broad research interest into a feasible question with methods and a clear host fit.

OppMatcher editorial · Evidence-first applicationsPublished 23 October 2026Updated 23 November 2026 with official sources

Turn a broad research interest into a feasible question with methods and a clear host fit. This is an evergreen decision guide. Always confirm the latest application status, scheme conditions and closing time on the official page.

Formulate a researchable question

A topic like climate change or artificial intelligence is too broad to show a realistic project. Identify a population, place, dataset or mechanism, and write a question that evidence could answer. Define why the problem is not already settled in the literature.

Choose methods you can carry out

Describe how you will collect or analyse evidence, what resources you need and what limitations you expect. A quantitative proposal should justify its data and analytic approach; a qualitative project should explain recruitment and ethical safeguards where relevant.

Explain why this institution fits

Name relevant facilities, research themes or academic expertise only if they appear in current official information. Avoid inventing a supervisor agreement. State what you would gain from the host and how you could contribute to its work.

Make the timeline and outputs plausible

Break the project into preparation, data collection, analysis and writing stages, with contingency for access or approvals. Specify a realistic output such as a thesis chapter, working paper or dataset. Fit the proposal to the provider's length and formatting instructions.

Practical example

Example: instead of 'I will study food security', propose an analysis of how storage conditions affect postharvest losses for a defined crop, location and measurement period, with a plan for finding usable data.

What to check

  1. Write one researchable question
  2. Verify methods and data access
  3. Align timeline with award length

Common question

Should I send the same research proposal to every scholarship?

A common outline may help, but the final proposal should reflect each funder's criteria, host research strengths, methods, timetable and required structure. Do not imply you have supervisor approval or data access unless that is confirmed.

Start from how the proposal will be graded

A scholarship research proposal is judged against criteria, and some funders publish them. The Commonwealth Scholarship Commission's selection criteria for doctoral scholarships grade the "quality of research proposal" on:

  • Primary indicators: a clear abstract of what you want to research, and the quality of the proposal, including innovation and relevance both to current research in the field and to development policy.
  • Secondary indicators: clarity of expression and feasibility of the proposed research. Earlier CSC criteria documents also list the likelihood of completion within three years.

The CSC's FAQ adds that Commonwealth Scholarships are graded for academic merit and development impact, with PhD applications also graded for the quality of the research proposal. Other funders weight things differently, but most ask some version of the same questions: is it clear, is it original, does it matter, and can you do it in the time available?

A structure that answers those questions

SectionWhat it must doShare of a 2,000-word proposal
Title and abstractState the question, method and expected contribution in plain language~150–200 words
Background and gapSummarise what is known, with key references, and name the specific gap~400 words
Research questions or aimsTwo or three focused, answerable questions~150 words
Methods and dataDesign, data sources, sample, analysis and why they suit the questions~550 words
Feasibility and timelineYear-by-year plan, access to data or field sites, ethics, risks~300 words
Significance and impactAcademic contribution and, where the funder asks, policy or development relevance~300 words
ReferencesOnly what you citeCheck whether they count towards the limit

Limits vary: some university studentships set 2,000 words including references and ask for a separate non-technical summary. Queen Mary University of London's humanities and social sciences guidance, for example, asks for 2,000 words plus a 200-word summary. Use the funder's limit and headings if it gives them. If it doesn't, the table above is a sensible default.

Worked example: from topic to answerable question

Too broad: "Climate change and agriculture in East Africa."

Better: "How do smallholder farmers in two Ugandan districts adjust planting dates in response to seasonal rainfall forecasts, and does forecast access change maize yields?"

Method that fits: a mixed-methods design combining household surveys in both districts (one with an existing forecast-dissemination programme, one without), district yield records over five seasons, and interviews with extension officers. The proposal then states how many households, how the districts were chosen, how yields will be compared, and the main limitation: the districts differ in ways other than forecast access, and the analysis will control for soil and rainfall.

The improved version tells a reviewer exactly what will be studied, where, how, and why it matters for policy on forecast services. That is the "innovation and relevance" and "feasibility" the CSC grades.

Supervisor fit and the institution

For PhD scholarships, show that the host can support the project. The CSC asks PhD applicants for a supporting statement from their proposed supervisor, uploaded before submission, and its older grading criteria mention the extent of engagement with the host supervisor. Contact potential supervisors early with a short summary, not the full proposal. Ask whether the topic fits their group, then revise the proposal to reflect their advice and the department's methods and facilities.

Proposal checklist

  1. Find the funder's criteria, limit and required headings.
  2. Write a one-sentence research question before anything else.
  3. Make sure every method choice answers a question in the proposal.
  4. Include a realistic timeline that fits the funded period.
  5. State the limitations and how you will handle them.
  6. Explain relevance in the funder's own terms, such as development impact for the CSC.
  7. Ask a supervisor or researcher in the field to read it before you submit.
