Scholarship Country Eligibility Matching: Avoid False Positive Results

Distinguish citizenship, place of residence and destination restrictions when using opportunity directories.

OppMatcher editorial · Evidence-first applicationsPublished 23 October 2026Updated 23 November 2026 with official sources

Distinguish citizenship, place of residence and destination restrictions when using opportunity directories. This is an evergreen decision guide. Always confirm the latest application status, scheme conditions and closing time on the official page.

Define the applicant profile accurately

A matching tool may need citizenship, residence, academic level and intended field. Those details describe different eligibility conditions. Do not treat the country where someone currently lives as equivalent to the passport they hold.

Classify the provider's rule type

Some scholarships accept all nationalities, some list individual countries, some use partner-country classifications and others require embassy nomination. Record whether a rule is explicitly verified, inferred or absent. Only verified restrictions should drive firm matching decisions.

Account for country-specific application routes

A programme can exist globally while one national application route is closed and another open. Check the exact country's provider page before declaring the scholarship available to a person. A rolling programme description does not prove a particular intake is active.

Explain uncertainty to the applicant

If nationality or course eligibility is missing, show 'needs verification' rather than promising a match. Link directly to the official provider and explain which condition has not been confirmed. Accurate uncertainty is more useful than a misleading 100% fit score.

Practical example

Example: a Pakistani national living in the United Kingdom looking at a Korean scholarship may qualify through a route based on nationality rather than UK residence. They must check the current government notice and its actual selection pathway.

What to check

  1. Record citizenship and residence separately
  2. Verify the correct country application channel
  3. Flag unknown requirements explicitly

Common question

Does the study destination determine whether my nationality is eligible?

No. Destination, citizenship, residence and nomination route are separate factors. Read the official eligible-country list for the current award and check whether applicants must apply through a country body.

"Open to my country" can mean four different tests

A scholarship listed as "open to applicants from Nigeria" may mean any of four quite different things, and schemes often combine them:

  1. Citizenship: the passport you hold.
  2. Residence: where you live now, or have lived permanently.
  3. Your parents' citizenship. Some schemes test this too.
  4. Where you apply from: which embassy, agency or country office handles your application.

Matching tools, including ours, can only compare the fields they hold. If a listing says "country-specific eligibility, see official page", that is deliberate: the official rule is more detailed than a single country tag.

How three major schemes apply these tests

SchemeCitizenshipResidence or routeOther nationality rules
CheveningCitizen of a Chevening-eligible country or territoryYou apply from your country of citizenship, regardless of where you live, and must commit to returning for at least two yearsDual UK citizens are not eligible
Commonwealth Master's (CSC)Citizen of an eligible Commonwealth country, or refugee status there, or British Protected PersonAlso permanently resident in an eligible Commonwealth country, and nominated through that country's agency or an approved NGO—
Global Korea Scholarship (GKS)Citizen of a country designated by NIIED for that year's programmeTrack-specific: embassy or university trackThe applicant and both parents must hold non-Korean citizenship. Anyone holding Korean citizenship alongside another is ineligible.

None of these is captured by "open to Nigeria". A Nigerian citizen living in the UK, for example, might be eligible for Chevening (which tests citizenship and applying from Nigeria) and not for a Commonwealth Master's (which also requires permanent residence in an eligible Commonwealth country).

Where false matches come from

  • Region labels. "Open to African students" may cover only a list of countries, or only some tracks.
  • Year-to-year lists. GKS's eligible countries and quotas are set in each year's guidelines. Chevening's list of eligible countries changes too.
  • Dual nationality. A second citizenship can make you ineligible (dual UK for Chevening, Korean for GKS), even if your first citizenship qualifies.
  • Residence versus citizenship. Living in an eligible country does not make you a citizen of it, and vice versa.
  • Old pages. An archived announcement may list countries that have since been removed.

Worked example: one applicant, three schemes

Amara holds Kenyan and Canadian citizenship and has lived in Toronto for five years. She checks three schemes:

  • Chevening: she would apply as a Kenyan citizen, from Kenya's programme, and commit to returning to Kenya for two years. She must confirm that her Canadian citizenship and Canadian residence don't create a problem under the current rules.
  • Commonwealth Master's: her Kenyan citizenship qualifies, but she is not permanently resident in Kenya. Canada is a high-income country, so it is not an eligible country of residence for this programme. She is likely ineligible.
  • GKS: both her citizenships are non-Korean and so are her parents'. Her eligibility then depends on whether Kenya or Canada is designated in that year's guidelines, and which embassy track she uses.

A tool matching on "Kenya" alone would mark all three as matches. Only one is clear-cut.

Country eligibility checklist

  1. Read the official eligibility page for the current year, not a listing.
  2. Check each test separately: citizenship, residence, parents' nationality, and the route you apply through.
  3. Check every citizenship you hold, including any acquired by birth or descent.
  4. Note any return-home or service commitment.
  5. If your case is unusual, email the provider and keep the reply.
