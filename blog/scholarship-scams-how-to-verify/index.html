How to verify a scholarship and avoid scams

Verify sponsors and application routes

How to approach it

A scholarship logo or message cannot establish that a sender represents the sponsoring organisation. Look up the programme on the organiser's own website and compare the award title, eligible countries and current submission route.

Treat requests for payments to release an award, account passwords or identity documents sent to personal email addresses as warning signs. An offer that appears only on an aggregator is not proof of an active official scholarship.

If the provider has not published the call, record it as unverified and contact the organiser using contact information from its own domain. Do not accept an urgency claim as proof of authenticity.

Two example offers: how the evidence differs

Example A: A social-media post promises a fully funded award “for everyone” with an immediate decision. The link goes to an unrelated domain, the application asks for a release payment and the supposed scholarship cannot be found on the named sponsor's own website. This is a high-risk example. Do not send money, passwords or identity documents to test it.

Example B: A post mentions a named award but points to a third-party article. You independently find the official sponsor's programme page, check the current application notice and confirm its own application platform. This is a better research route, but a real sponsor page still does not guarantee the specific call is open or that you qualify.

These are illustrative situations, not reports about named organisations. The important distinction is whether the opportunity can be checked independently through the provider's controlled channels.

Check domain ownership without trusting the appearance

Read the full website address before entering personal details. A link such as example-university.fake-apply.net is controlled by fake-apply.net, not by the university name shown on the left. Even a site using HTTPS can be fraudulent; the padlock means an encrypted connection, not that its claims are true.

Type the provider's known website independently, use its navigation to find the programme, and follow the application link from there. Do not depend on a search advertisement, direct message or a screenshot as the only evidence. Where possible, compare the programme title, application date and contact address with the official announcement.

Scholarship aggregators can be useful for discovering names, but they are secondary sources. If an organiser hasn't published the current call, mark the opportunity as unconfirmed and do not assume a previous year's deadline carries forward.

If you've already shared information

  1. Stop communicating with the suspicious sender and don't make further payments.
  2. If you've disclosed a password, change it through the official service and enable multi-factor authentication. Don't share one-time verification codes.
  3. If payment details were involved, contact your bank using its official website or number and ask about protection or reporting options.
  4. Keep the message, URL, payment reference and screenshots for reporting, but avoid publishing your passport or personal details.
  5. Report impersonation to the real organisation through an independently verified contact channel. Reporting options differ by country.

There is no universal rule that every scholarship application is free; some legitimate programmes involve university application charges. What matters is whether a charge is disclosed by the real provider and paid through its approved system. Treat demands for personal transfers or an award-release fee as serious warning signs.
