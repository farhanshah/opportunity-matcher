How to find the right programme

Europe contains several different labour and student mobility systems. A paid placement in Switzerland, an EU-funded traineeship and an internship at a UK employer may have completely different residence, payroll and visa conditions.

Begin with the official vacancy. Check whether the employer recruits applicants outside the host country and whether the role is open to current students or graduates. The word European does not itself establish eligibility for non-EU citizens.

For research placements, check academic year and institutional sponsorship. For corporate roles, match the CV to the named requisition and document any work permit that the employer requires.

Paid institutional internships in Europe

The best-paid internships in Europe that are open to international applicants are run by the EU institutions. The largest is the European Commission's Blue Book Traineeship, which takes about 1,000 trainees twice a year, with a limited number of places for non-EU nationals.

ItemBlue Book Traineeship (European Commission)
Length and start datesFive months, starting on 1 March and 1 October each year
PayA monthly grant of about 1,538 euros for living costs
EducationA completed higher education degree (a three-year degree or equivalent); recent calls also opened it to vocational graduates
LanguagesVery good knowledge of two EU languages, one of which must be English, French or German
Prior experienceNo more than six weeks of previous work in any EU institution or body
DeadlinesFor the March 2027 session, applications closed on 4 September 2026; applications for the October session usually open early in the year

Other routes

  • Other EU institutions and agencies, such as the European Parliament and EU agencies, run their own traineeships with their own calls and conditions.
  • Erasmus+ traineeships are for students already enrolled at a European university and are arranged through that university. See our Erasmus+ guide.
  • Company internships follow national rules on pay and visas, which differ between countries.

Applying as a non-EU national

Non-EU applicants compete for a small number of places in specific departments, so the application should show a clear link between your background and the Commission's work, for example trade, development or digital policy. Apply through EU Login and complete every section, because incomplete applications are not considered.

Example: A hypothetical Indonesian graduate with a law degree and fluent English and French meets the language rule and could apply for a Blue Book session. Someone with English and Urdu only would not meet the two-EU-language rule.

What to check before applying

  1. Identify the latest official programme or individual vacancy announcement.
  2. Check applicant citizenship, residence, course or career stage and nomination requirements.
  3. Record exact deadlines, documents, conditions and funding from the current source.
