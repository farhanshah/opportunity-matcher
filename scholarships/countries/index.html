Study destination and applicant nationality are separate questions

Start with the country where you want to study, then check whether a named award accepts people with your citizenship and residence history. A scholarship for study in the United Kingdom can have an approved-country list; a Japanese university may offer scholarships through an embassy or university route; and an Australian award can apply to selected partner countries. None of those distinctions can be inferred from the destination name alone.

Check your applicant country separately

A scholarship offered in a country may be open to specific citizenships, residents or nominating-country groups. Read the official call and check nationality, current residence, academic level and eligible university course. The place of study does not determine the country from which you should apply.

Follow the route that applies to your own passport and location

Imagine two hypothetical students planning a master's degree in the same destination. One holds a passport accepted by a government scholarship; the other does not, but may still qualify for a university's separate merit award. The same study destination does not imply the same funding options. Compare the applicant criteria before deciding which form to prepare.

Your application route can also depend on where you currently live. Some programmes ask for a country-specific embassy application, a sending-partner nomination or a university application submitted in an earlier window. Residency and citizenship are different facts, so record them separately. Do not assume the country where you attend university is automatically the country under which the scholarship must be requested.

What to record when comparing countries

  1. Write the destination and exact programme title, and save the official programme URL.
  2. Copy the sponsor's nationality and residence restrictions verbatim, including exceptions.
  3. Identify degree level, acceptable institutions and any field-of-study or language requirements.
  4. Record what is actually funded: tuition, stipend, travel, insurance and dependants if stated.
  5. Separately record university admission, nomination, scholarship and referee deadlines with time zones.
  6. Only treat applications as open if the current official intake specifically says so.

Budget example: A tuition waiver with no maintenance award can leave housing and insurance unpaid. A stipend with partial tuition can also leave a substantial gap. Compare costs by actual line item, rather than sorting by the word “fully funded.” Our funding comparison guide explains how to build that worksheet.
